# Product

## Register

product

## Users

People who use Marinara Engine for roleplay and character chat, who open Wanderworld to walk around a world made from their own characters, persona and lorebooks. Most are not technical, many play on a phone, and they are in the middle of playing when they open a menu: they want to change something and get back to the world.

A smaller group likes to tune everything: fights by the 5e rules, the AI's prompts, how many creatures there are, what each setting does exactly. They should find every number and rule, but behind the (i) or in Help, not on the screen everyone else reads.

So the text is layered. The label and the line on screen are plain and short for anyone. The full detail sits one tap away.

## Product Purpose

Wanderworld is a 3D world on its own Home tab in Marinara Engine. The player walks a town and the country round it, talks to the people in it, fights, gathers, trades, and starts Engine scenes from where they are. The world is built from the player's lore, and the AI can narrate, voice people and decide things, but every part also works without the AI.

The menus (Settings, Inventory, Journal, Lore, Help, the fight bar, the New world card) exist to serve play. Success is a player who reads a label or a line once, knows what it does, and goes back to the world. A setting that needs explaining has its explanation behind an (i), and that explanation says what On does and what Off does.

## Brand Personality

plain, quiet, exact

The voice says exactly what a thing does, in short sentences and common words, with British spelling. It doesn't sell, cheer or hedge. The world itself carries the atmosphere (the narration, people's lines, the fights); the menus stay out of its way. Warmth comes from being clear and from respecting the player's time, not from exclamation marks.

## Anti-references

The text should never read like:

- **AI-written filler.** Padding, hedging, "seamlessly", "whether you're…", lists of three for rhythm, cheerful sign-offs, restating what was just said.
- **Developer settings.** Jargon, internal names and code-speak (tokens, ids, prompts, routes) shown to a player who didn't ask for them. Where a technical word is needed, it is explained once in plain words.
- **A tabletop rulebook.** Long rule paragraphs on the screen itself. The rule's numbers belong behind the (i) or in Help.
- **Marketing copy.** "Powerful", "immersive", "unleash": selling a feature instead of saying what it does.

## Design Principles

1. **Say what it does, then stop.** A label names the thing. A line says what it does. An explanation says what On does and what Off does. Nothing more on the screen.
2. **Layer the detail.** Plain by default; numbers, rules and edge cases one tap away behind the (i), and the whole story in Help. Shorter must never mean vaguer: every fact a setting carries stays somewhere the player can reach it.
3. **Works without the AI, and says so.** Every feature has a non-AI path. Text about an AI feature says what it costs (one call each time) and what happens when it's off or fails.
4. **Everything switchable, nothing surprising.** Each feature can be turned off, and its text makes the switch's effect plain. Hand-set values are respected and can be locked.
5. **One term for each thing.** The same word everywhere for the same thing (a setting's label is quoted exactly wherever it's referred to), and the setting's own words (from the setting pack) where the world has its own.

## Accessibility & Inclusion

- **Phones first.** Labels short enough for a narrow screen. Nothing needs hover to be understood: the (i) opens on a tap, and a tooltip only repeats what can be read elsewhere.
- **Screen readers.** Every button and control has a name that makes sense read aloud on its own ("Cast Fire Bolt", not "Cast"; "What this does" for the (i)), and an icon-only control always has a label.
